John's Pass

A narrow cut with a swift tidal current and shoals that shift after storms. Take it slow, mind the markers, and do not cut the corners.

Shifting sand and shoals that move with the seasons. Yesterday's safe line may not be today's - ease in and read the water.

Freighters and cruise ships run this road and cannot slow down or turn for a small boat. Stay clear of the big hulls and cross with care.

A narrow, winding pass where shoaling sneaks up between markers. Slow down and trust your depth sounder over your memory.

Strong current rips under the bridge while shoals reach out from both ends. Time it near slack water if you can.

The Skyway on a Strong Tide

Open water under the bridge stacks up steep and close together when a hard tide runs against the wind. If it looks angry, wait it out.
Tricky water

Hurricane Pass

Strong current pours through this narrow cut and the sandbars shift - some dry out completely at low water. Follow the marked channel and expect company on weekends.
Tricky water

Clearwater Pass

A busy dredged entrance with shoals crowding both sides of the cut. Stay in the marked channel, mind the tour-boat traffic, and do not feel your way in after dark.
Tricky water

Southwest Channel

The quieter second way into the bay crosses a bar that loves to shoal. It is lightly marked, breakers can stand near Passage Key, and old wrecks sit in the entrance - work the sounder and take it slow.
Tricky water

Pass-a-Grille Channel

The dredged run behind Pass-a-Grille looks wide open, but skinny flats press in on both sides. Hold the markers, especially at low water - outside the cut it shallows up fast.
Tricky water

The Narrows in Boca Ciega Bay

A tight, walled-in stretch of the Intracoastal with rocks hiding under the surface near the south end. Favor the east bank, hold your line, and expect close-quarters passes.
Tricky water

Cockroach Bay & the Little Manatee Mouth

A broad, skinny bay of grass flats and mangrove edges where the river meets the bay. Water that looks plenty deep often is not - work the creeks on a rising tide and go slow over the flats.

General local knowledge only - always check current NOAA charts and the Local Notice to Mariners before you run unfamiliar water.

Boat Maintenance

The routine that keeps you off the towboat's hook.

>
1After every trip

Flush the engine with fresh water for 5 minutes after every saltwater run — it saves thousands. Wash the boat and trailer, check the bilge, wipe down vinyl before the sun bakes the salt in.

2Monthly

Check battery charge and clean terminals — a dead battery strands more boaters than anything else. Add fuel stabilizer if she sits more than a few weeks. Check safety gear expiry dates, inspect dock lines and fenders.

3Yearly

Full engine service, replace sacrificial anodes, inspect trailer bearings and tires, check fire extinguishers, and refresh bottom paint if she lives in a slip.

4Florida extras

Salt corrosion never sleeps here — rinse religiously. Have a hurricane haul-out plan ready before June. And cover the boat: the Florida sun destroys vinyl and gelcoat fast.

The 5-minute rule

If you only do one thing, flush the engine after every saltwater trip. It is the cheapest insurance in boating.

Boat Troubleshooting

Basic diagnostics for the problems that ruin a day on the water — outboards, inboards, bilge pumps and electrics. Start with the simple checks; most breakdowns are something small.

>
Stop first if: you smell raw fuel in the bilge (do NOT start the engine — open hatches, ventilate, find the leak), water is rising faster than the pump handles, or anything is smoking. Those aren’t diagnostics, they’re emergencies — see Boating Safety.
Outboard

Cranks but won’t start

Check first: Kill-switch lanyard clipped in, shifter in neutral, fuel-tank vent open, primer bulb squeezed firm.
Also: Fuel actually in the tank (gauges lie), water in the fuel/water separator, fouled spark plugs.
Call a pro: No spark at any plug, or the warning horn sounds.
Outboard

No tell-tale stream, or it overheats

Check first: The pee-hole outlet is plugged — clear it gently with a piece of wire or heavy fishing line. Intake screens on the lower unit blocked by grass or a plastic bag.
Also: Weak stream usually means a worn water-pump impeller.
Call a pro: The temp alarm keeps sounding. Shut it down — running hot kills powerheads.
Outboard & Inboard

Won’t crank — just clicks

Check first: Battery switch ON, terminals tight and clean (try to twist them by hand — if they move, that’s your problem), battery charged.
Also: Jiggle the shifter in neutral — a worn neutral safety switch blocks the starter.
Call a pro: Burnt smell at the starter, or a new battery doesn’t fix it.
Inboard

Overheats, especially at speed

Check first: Raw-water seacock fully open, sea strainer basket clogged with grass.
Also: Raw-water pump impeller, loose pump belt, coolant level if it has closed cooling (check only when the engine is cold).
Call a pro: Steam from the engine box, or coolant showing up in the bilge.
Any Engine

Loses power or bogs down

Check first: The prop — engine revs but the boat won’t go means a spun hub; fishing line or damage on the prop does the same. Heavy growth on the hull drags speed too.
Also: Fuel/water separator full of water, clogged fuel filter, kinked fuel line, tank vent closed.
Call a pro: Power loss with knocking, smoke, or a miss that won’t clear.
Any Engine

Rough idle, stalls at the dock

Check first: Old or water-contaminated fuel — ethanol fuel goes bad fast in Florida heat.
Also: Fouled plugs, an air leak in the fuel line (primer bulb won’t stay firm), dirty idle circuits from sitting.
Call a pro: It stalls going into gear — that’s a safety problem around docks and ramps, not a quirk.
Bilge

Pump won’t run

Check first: The fuse, then lift the float switch by hand — if the pump kicks on, the float was stuck or blocked by debris.
Also: Pump screen clogged, and the pump’s wiring — bilge connections are the first on the boat to corrode.
Know this: Every boat should have TWO ways to move water: the electric pump and a manual backup.
Bilge

Water keeps coming in

Check first: Drain plug in? Through-hull fittings and their hoses, livewell intakes, and on sterndrives the rubber bellows — cracked bellows sink boats at the dock.
If it’s rising and you can’t find it: Life jackets on, call for help early (see Safety), and head for shallow water or the nearest shore. Don’t wait it out.
Electrical

Batteries keep dying

Check first: Battery switch left on, corroded or loose terminals, something left running — blower, lights, stereo amp.
Also: Loose or glazed alternator belt (engine runs, battery never charges), and plain battery age — Florida heat eats batteries in a few seasons.
Call a pro: A battery that’s hot, swollen, or smells like rotten eggs. That’s overcharging, and it’s dangerous.
Steering & Trim

Stiff steering, or trim won’t move

Check first: Trim/tilt fluid level. When you hit the switch: a click means the solenoids work and the motor is the suspect; no click points to a fuse or relay.
Also: Low hydraulic steering fluid, and cable steering stiffening from corrosion — never force it.
Call a pro: Any looseness or play in the steering. That’s a no-go until it’s fixed.
Still stuck? These are the basic checks a handy owner can do safely. Beyond that, a good marine mechanic earns his money — find one in Marine Services, and keep breakdowns rare with the Maintenance checklists.

Tampa Bay Boating History

From dugout canoes to the Skyway — how this bay became a boating town. Every fact below was checked against museum, state and federal sources; the stories we couldn’t verify are labeled as the legends they are.

>
Before the 1500s

The first boaters

People have lived on Tampa Bay for at least 14,500 years. The Tocobaga chiefdom’s capital stood at the north end of Old Tampa Bay — today’s Safety Harbor — and their boats were dugouts hollowed from single tree trunks. To the south, the Calusa ran a maritime society in cypress dugouts and ranged as far as Cuba.
1528–1539

Spain arrives by sea

Pánfilo de Narváez landed near the bay in April 1528 hunting gold that wasn’t here; starving, his men built makeshift barges and most were lost at sea. In May 1539 Hernando de Soto came ashore and named the bay Bahía de Espíritu Santo — Bay of the Holy Spirit. De Soto National Memorial in Bradenton marks the shore historians believe he landed on.
c. 1575

Why it’s called “Tampa”

The earliest written form is “Tanpa,” in a Spanish memoir from about 1575 — and it actually named a place farther south; the name drifted north over time. Its original meaning is lost. You’ll hear it means “sticks of fire” — a popular translation historians say can’t be verified.
1824

Fort Brooke: a port is born

The Army planted Fort Brooke on the Hillsborough River in January 1824, and a wharf followed by 1829. Tampa grew up around the fort’s trade and protection, and more than 40,000 soldiers passed through during the Second Seminole War.
1848

The Great Gale

That September, the strongest storm in the bay’s recorded history drove the water up about 15 feet in a few hours, left only five houses standing in Tampa, and battered the Egmont Key lighthouse, lit just that year. Local tradition says the gale tore open the pass we now call John’s Pass, and that turtle fisherman John Levique was first to sail through it — a story told ever since, though never documented.
1848–1858

A lighthouse for the bay

Egmont Key’s first lighthouse lit in 1848 — the only light between St. Marks and Key West, guarding the ship channel into Tampa and St. Petersburg. Storms wrecked it; the tower standing today was built in 1857–58. In the late 1850s the island held captured Seminoles awaiting removal west — Chief Billy Bowlegs is believed to have been among them.
1858

Cattle boats to Cuba

Capt. James McKay opened Tampa’s cattle trade with Cuba in 1858, loading herds on the Interbay Peninsula — the Spanish paid in gold doubloons. In the 1870s alone, more than 165,000 head of Florida cattle shipped out to Cuban ports.
1884

Plant changes everything

Henry Plant’s railroad reached Tampa in 1884, and by 1889 his deep-water Port Tampa terminal had steamships running to Havana, Mobile and Jamaica — including the U.S. mail run to Key West and Havana. His Tampa Bay Hotel, a quarter-mile long, opened in 1891.
1886

Cigar City floats in

Vicente Ybor built his first cigar factory and worker houses in 1886. The tobacco leaf came by steamship from Cuba — Plant’s Olivette ran the Tampa–Key West–Havana route — and the finished cigars left by rail. The combination made Tampa the cigar capital of the world into the 1930s.
1898

The Army sails from Tampa

For the Spanish-American War, Tampa was America’s port of embarkation: over 30,000 troops staged here, including Theodore Roosevelt’s Rough Riders, and some 16,000 sailed from Port Tampa for Cuba. The Tampa Bay Hotel served as headquarters.
1904

Gasparilla!

Ye Mystic Krewe of Gasparilla staged Tampa’s first pirate invasion in May 1904 — on horseback. The invasion took to the water in 1911, the Krewe got a ship of its own in 1937, and the purpose-built Jose Gasparilla II debuted in 1954.
The legend: José Gaspar himself almost certainly never existed — historians find no evidence for him. The tale traces to an old-timer’s stories around 1900, dressed up to launch a festival. Tampa loved it too much to let it go.
1905

The sponge divers

Tarpon Springs hooked sponges from boats from about 1890. In 1905 Greek immigrant John Cocoris brought over Mediterranean helmet divers — within a year some 500 Greek divers were working about 50 boats. The Sponge Exchange opened in 1908; a sponge disease in 1939 shrank the beds, but the Sponge Docks still work today.
1914

The first airline was a flying boat

On January 1, 1914, pilot Tony Jannus lifted a Benoist flying boat off St. Petersburg’s Central Yacht Basin and flew to Tampa — the world’s first scheduled commercial airline flight. The 18-mile hop took about 23 minutes, versus up to 12 hours by rail, and the line carried some 1,200 passengers that winter.
1921

The hurricane that remade the coast

On October 25, 1921, a Category 3 hurricane came ashore near Tarpon Springs: a ten-and-a-half-foot tide drowned Bayshore, smashed steamers against Tampa’s docks, and killed eight people. The surge cut Hog Island in two — the gap is Hurricane Pass, and the halves are today’s Honeymoon and Caladesi islands.
1924–1954

Ferries, bridges, and rum-runners

The Gandy Bridge opened in 1924 and cut the Tampa–St. Pete drive from 43 miles to 19. The Davis Causeway — today’s Courtney Campbell — followed in 1934. On the water, the Bee Line Ferry ran from Pinellas Point to Piney Point from 1926 until the Skyway replaced it in 1954. And in 1924 the Coast Guard set up at St. Pete’s Bayboro Harbor to chase Prohibition rum-runners.
1934

The Coast Guard takes the sky

Coast Guard Air Station St. Petersburg was commissioned at Albert Whitted Airport in 1934, flying search-and-rescue over the Gulf. In 1976 it moved to St. Pete–Clearwater airport and became Air Station Clearwater; the old Albert Whitted site is Coast Guard Sector St. Petersburg today.
1941–45

Tampa builds warships

Tampa Shipbuilding became the city’s biggest employer — some 16,000 workers — turning out Navy minesweepers, destroyer escorts and cargo ships. At MacDill Field, B-26 Marauder training crashes were so frequent locals coined a grim saying: “One a day in Tampa Bay.” The state museum counts 63 B-26s lost in the bay in 1943 alone.
1954

The Skyway

The original Sunshine Skyway opened September 6, 1954 — ending the ferry era and tying St. Petersburg to the mainland by road for good.
1963–1979

Sailboat capital of America

Fort De Soto Park was dedicated in 1963 around a Spanish-American War fort that never fired a shot in battle. Meanwhile local builders made Tampa Bay the sailboat capital of the country: Morgan Yachts (St. Pete, 1965 — its Out Island 41 became the charter-fleet standard, over 1,000 built), Gulfstar (1970, 700 workers at its peak) and Island Packet (Largo, 1979). The St. Petersburg Power & Sailboat Show launched in 1978. As builder Brad Lazzara put it: “This was the sailboat capital of the U.S.”
1980

The bay’s hardest year

On January 28, the Coast Guard cutter Blackthorn collided with the tanker Capricorn near the Skyway, capsized and sank — 23 of her 50 crew were lost, the Coast Guard’s worst peacetime disaster. Just over three months later, on the morning of May 9, the freighter Summit Venture hit the Skyway’s southbound span in a storm. Six cars, a truck and a Greyhound bus fell with the bridge. Thirty-five people died.
1987–Today

The new Skyway and the modern bay

The cable-stayed Bob Graham Sunshine Skyway opened April 20, 1987, its main piers ringed by giant concrete “dolphins” built to stop a ship. The old bridge’s approaches became Skyway Fishing Pier State Park in 1994 — billed as the world’s longest fishing pier, open around the clock. Lazzara Yachts started building in Tampa in 1990. Port Tampa Bay is now Florida’s largest port by tonnage and acreage, moving over 30 million tons a year. And in 2022 Florida passed one million registered boats — more than any other state — with Pinellas and Hillsborough both in the state’s top five.
Working Water

Cortez and the stone crab

The village of Cortez, settled in the 1880s by fishing families from North Carolina, still works mullet boats out of Sarasota Bay — one of Florida’s last historic fishing villages. Florida’s stone crab fishery dates to 1902; crabbers take a claw and return the crab alive to grow it back. Stone crab season runs October 15 to May 1.
Featured River

The Alafia — the river that glowed

The name story: Local tradition says Alafia is a Native word meaning “River of Fire” — the river was said to glow at night from the phosphate in its waters. No written record proves what the name means, but the story has been told on this river for generations, and we’re not about to stop telling it — labeled for what it is.
The river’s own history: Native peoples lived along it for thousands of years; the Mocoso and Tocobaga held its mouth. The Army built Fort Alafia in 1849, and by 1867 a ferry crossed at Peru, near today’s Riverview. Phosphate was found on the river in 1891 — barges hauled it down to the bay, a tug ran supplies from Gibsonton up to the mines near Mulberry, and Riverview was platted that same year. Lithia got its name in 1904 from the lithium in its spring water — Lithia Springs still pours some 35 million gallons a day into the river at a constant 72 degrees. And Alafia River State Park is the old Lonesome Mine: 6,312 acres of phosphate pits donated to the state in 1996 and opened as a park in 1998.
Sources: Tampa Bay History Center · Florida Memory (State Library of Florida) · Florida State Parks · National Park Service · Smithsonian · National Weather Service · U.S. Coast Guard · FWC · Hillsborough County. Registration & Titles

Florida boat paperwork without the headache — who has to register, how a private sale works, what it costs, and exactly what to bring to the Tax Collector.

The Basics

Who has to register

Every motorized boat used on Florida’s public waters must be titled and registered. Non-motorized boats 16 feet and longer must register too. You’re exempt if the boat is non-motor-powered and under 16 feet (canoes, kayaks, racing shells and rowing sculls are exempt at any length), used only on private lakes and ponds, a ship’s lifeboat, or owned by the U.S. Government. Federally documented vessels skip the Florida title but still register and carry a Florida decal. Everything runs through your county Tax Collector — they’re the state’s agent for boats. Titles are filed in person; renewals can be done online, by mail, or at the office.
Buying Used

The 30-day clock

1. The seller must hand you the Florida title, completed by both of you — signatures, purchase price, date of sale.
2. Get a bill of sale too (form HSMV 82050 covers vessels).
3. File form HSMV 82040 VS at the Tax Collector within 30 days of the sale — file late and state law adds a $10 penalty.
4. During those 30 days, keep proof of the purchase date aboard. After day 30, running unregistered is a second-degree misdemeanor.
5. Sales tax is due on the purchase price — 6% state tax, plus a county surtax where one applies (Pinellas adds 1% on the first $5,000). Titling runs $8.25 all-in, plus $1 for each lien recorded.
Checklist

What to bring to the Tax Collector

The Florida title, completed by buyer and seller · Bill of sale or invoice · Form HSMV 82040 VS, signed by each applicant (or their power of attorney) · Photo ID for each applicant — Florida driver license or ID card, passport, or U.S. military ID · Payment for title, registration and any sales tax owed.
Buying new instead? Bring the Manufacturer’s Statement of Origin and the dealer invoice. Boat from another state? Bring that state’s title — or its registration if it’s a non-title state — plus the bill of sale.
Renewals

The birthday rule

Florida registrations run on your birthday: for an individually owned boat the registration year starts the first day of your birth month, and your renewal window is the 30 days ending at midnight on your date of birth (the first-named owner’s birthday controls if there are two of you). Pick a 1-year or 2-year registration. Company boats run July 1–June 30 instead. Keep the registration certificate on the boat — the law requires it be aboard whenever the boat is operating. Hillsborough renewals: online portal, Express Lane pickup, mail, or the 24/7 drop boxes — links below.
On the Hull

Numbers & the decal

Your FL numbers go on both sides of the forward half of the hull, in block characters at least 3 inches tall, in a color that contrasts with the hull, reading left to right. The numbers stay with the boat for life. Knot 1 · To a hook

Palomar Knot — the easy strong one

Best for: hooks and lures — the strongest easy knot there is.

1. Fold the line in half so you have a loop.
2. Push the loop through the hook's eye.
3. Tie a simple overhand knot with the loop — don't pull it tight yet.
4. Drop the hook through the loop, wet the line, pull tight. Snip the extra.
Knot 2 · To a hook

Improved Clinch Knot — the classic

Best for: hooks and swivels — the classic everyone learns first.

1. Push the line through the hook's eye, leaving a hand-length of extra line.
2. Wrap the extra end around the main line 5 times.
3. Push the end through the little loop by the eye, then through the big loop you just made.
4. Wet it, pull tight slowly, snip the extra.
Knot 3 · To a hook

Uni Knot — the one that does everything

Best for: almost anything — hooks, swivels, even joining two lines.

1. Push the line through the eye and fold it back so the line doubles up.
2. Make a loop with the extra end, held against the doubled line.
3. Wrap the end through the loop and around both lines 4 to 6 times.
4. Wet it, pull the end to snug the wraps, then slide the knot down to the eye and pull the main line tight.
Knot 4 · To a hook

Trilene Knot — through the eye twice

Best for: hooks and swivels — extra strong, because the line goes through the eye twice.

1. Push the line through the hook’s eye TWICE, so it makes a double loop at the eye.
2. Wrap the loose end around the main line 5 to 6 times.
3. Push the end back through the double loop at the eye.
4. Wet it, pull tight slowly, snip the extra.
Knot 5 · To a hook

San Diego Jam — the strong one

Best for: big hooks and heavy lures — one of the strongest hook knots there is.

1. Push the line through the eye and bring the end back so it lies alongside the main line.
2. Wrap the end around BOTH lines 5 to 7 times, working up away from the hook.
3. Push the end through the little gap by the eye, then through the loop the wraps made.
4. Wet it, pull tight slowly, snip the extra.
Knot 6 · To a hook

Snell Knot — made for circle hooks

Best for: circle hooks — it pulls dead straight, so the hook sets in the corner of the mouth like it should.

1. Push the line through the eye and back through it again, leaving a long loop lying along the hook’s shank.
2. Hold the loop against the shank and wrap it around the shank and the line 5 to 7 times, working down the shank.
3. Hold the wraps in place and pull the main line slowly — the wraps snug down tight on the shank.
4. Pull it fully tight and snip the extra.
Knot 7 · For lures

Non-Slip Loop Knot — lets lures swim

Best for: lures — the open loop lets a lure swim and dart like it’s alive.

1. Tie a simple overhand knot in the line a few inches from the end — leave it loose.
2. Push the end through the lure’s eye, then back through the loose overhand knot.
3. Wrap the end around the main line 3 to 4 times.
4. Push the end through the overhand knot one more time, wet it, and pull the main line tight — leave a small loop at the lure.
Knot 8 · Line to line

Double Uni Knot — line to line

Best for: joining two lines — main line to leader, or braid to leader.

1. Lay the two lines side by side, overlapping by about a hand’s length.
2. With one loose end, form a loop and wrap it around BOTH lines 4 to 5 times, then pull that knot snug (not fully tight).
3. Do the exact same knot with the other loose end, facing the other way.
4. Wet both knots, then pull the two main lines apart — the knots slide together and lock. Snip the loose ends close.
Knot 9 · Line to line

Blood Knot — the slim join

Best for: joining two lines of about the same thickness — smooth and slim.

1. Cross the two lines so they overlap by about a hand’s length, like an X.
2. Wrap one loose end around the other line 5 times, then poke it back through the gap in the middle.
3. Wrap the other loose end around the first line 5 times the other way, and poke it through the same middle gap from the opposite side.
4. Wet it and pull both main lines slowly — the wraps stack up neatly. Snip the loose ends close.
Knot 10 · To the reel

Arbor Knot — line to reel

Best for: tying fresh line onto a reel spool so it doesn’t spin.

1. Wrap the line once around the spool and tie a simple overhand knot around the main line. Snip that extra close.
2. Tie a second overhand knot in the loose end itself — it acts as a stopper.
3. Pull the main line so both knots slide down and snug against the spool.
4. Start winding the reel — the line grips the spool and loads up tight.

Never fished before? Start at Step 1 and go in order. By Step 12 you have caught a fish.

Step 1 · Before you go

Grown-ups need a license

Kids under 16 fish free — no license. Grown-ups need a Florida saltwater fishing license, and fishing without one can get you a ticket. The license card at the top of this page shows how to get one.
Step 2 · Your rod

Start with a simple spinning combo

A spinning rod and reel that come together in one package. Medium size is right for Tampa Bay. Any tackle shop can spool it with line for you. It's the easiest setup there is to learn on.
Step 3 · Tie your hook on

Use the Palomar knot

One knot is all you need to start: the Palomar. It's the strongest easy knot there is.

See the Palomar knot steps →
Step 4 · Add a bobber

Your bite alarm

Clip a bobber onto your line about 2 feet above the hook, with a small split-shot weight pinched on under it. The bobber floats on top. When a fish bites, the bobber goes under. That's your signal.
Step 5 · Bait the hook

Start with shrimp

Thread a small piece of shrimp onto the hook so the point just pokes out the other side. Shrimp is Tampa Bay's #1 beginner bait — almost everything here eats it.
Step 6 · Cast it out

Smooth, not hard

Point the rod where you want the bait to land. Swing it forward in one smooth motion and let the line fly off. Whipping it hard just tangles it. Smooth beats strong.
Step 7 · Wait and watch

Watch the bobber

Let the bait sit. Little bounces mean little fish are nibbling. When the bobber goes all the way under and stays under — that's a real bite. Get ready.
Step 8 · Set the hook

One quick snap

Lift the rod tip up fast — one quick snap of the wrist. That sticks the hook in. Firm, not violent: you're setting a hook, not starting a lawn mower.
Step 9 · Reel it in

Steady wins

Reel in steady and keep the rod tip up. If the fish pulls hard, stop reeling and let it pull for a second, then reel again. Never yank — yanking tears the hook out.
Step 10 · Land your fish

You caught one

Slide a net under the fish, or lift it in one smooth motion. Wet your hands before you touch it — dry hands rub off the slime that protects the fish's skin.
Step 11 · Take the hook out

Pliers make it easy

Hold the fish gently and push the hook out backwards with pliers — the way it went in. If a fish swallowed the hook deep and you're letting it go, cut the line close to the hook instead. The hook rusts away.
Step 12 · Keep it or let it go

Check the size first

Keeping it? Measure it and check the Fish Chart — a fish that's too small has to go back, it's the law. Keepers go straight on ice. Letting it go? Hold it in the water, move it slowly back and forth, and let it swim out of your hands on its own.

You caught it — now make it dinner. Go slow. A sharp knife does the work for you.

First · The law

Size-limit fish stay whole until you’re home

A fish with a size limit has to stay whole — head, tail and all — until you're back on land. Filleting one on the boat or at the ramp can get you a ticket. Fillet at home.
You need

Your tools

A sharp fillet knife, a cutting board, pliers, a bowl of clean water, zip bags and a cooler of ice. A dull knife means hard work and cut fingers — sharp is safer.
Step 1

Ice it right away

The moment you decide to keep a fish, it goes on ice. A cold fish fillets cleaner and tastes ten times better.
Step 2

Rinse it and lay it flat

Rinse the slime off. Lay the fish flat on the board — right-handed, head pointing left. Keep your holding hand curled, fingers tucked back out of the knife's path.
Step 3

First cut — behind the gill

Cut down right behind the gill plate until the knife touches the backbone. Stop there — don't cut through the backbone.
Step 4

Slide along the backbone

Turn the knife flat, pointing toward the tail. Slide it along the top of the backbone all the way to the tail in long, smooth strokes. Let the bones steer the blade.
Step 5

Take the meat off the skin

Lay the fillet skin-side down. Grab the tail end of the skin with pliers. Hold the skin tight and slide the knife flat between the skin and the meat, cutting away from you.
Step 6

Trim and check for bones

Cut out the dark red strip down the center — that's the strong, fishy taste. Run your fingertips over the fillet: any little bones you feel, pull them out with pliers.
Step 7

Rinse, bag, ice

Rinse the fillet quick, pat it dry, and seal it in a bag. Bury the bag in ice. Eat it within 2 days — or freeze it the day you catch it.
Whole fish instead?

Cleaning a fish whole

Cut the belly open from the tail to the gills and pull the guts out. Cooking it with the skin on? Scrape the scales off with the back of a spoon, tail to head. Rinse inside and out, then ice it.

How This Works

1. Pick your water — each tab above is a different kind of fishing: Ocean, Bays, Rivers, Lakes (ponds are in the Lakes tab). The fish, the rod and the bait change with the water.

2. One rod covers most of it — a 7 ft medium spinning combo handles the bay, the rivers and most lake fishing. The Gear tab tells you exactly what to buy.

3. Get legal — Florida has TWO licenses: saltwater and freshwater, and you need the one that matches your water. Kids under 16 fish free. Freshwater

Lakes & Ponds

What you'll catch: largemouth bass year-round, bluegill everywhere, black crappie ("specks") in the cooler months, channel catfish all day, plus the occasional bowfin and gar.

The lakes: Lake Tarpon (Palm Harbor) is the famous big-bass lake. Also Lake Seminole, Lake Maggiore, Lake Thonotosassa, Lake Carroll, Lake Rogers and Crescent Lake — most have bank fishing and park access, no boat required.

Ponds: neighborhood and retention ponds all over the area hold bass, bluegill and catfish — some of the easiest fishing there is. Public park ponds are fair game; if a pond is posted, gated or behind a community's signs, you need permission. Many park ponds are catch-and-release — read the park sign.

Rod: one 6'6" to 7 ft medium spinning combo, 8–12 lb line. For ponds, a light 6–6.5 ft combo with 6–8 lb line is even more fun.

Bait: live shiners are the #1 Florida bass bait (under a bobber), red worms and crickets for bluegill, small minnows and jigs for crappie, plastic worms and spinnerbaits for lures. Lakes need the freshwater license.

Rods & bait

One Rod Covers Freshwater

The lake rod: one 6'6" to 7 ft medium spinning combo with 8–12 lb line. For ponds, a light 6–6.5 ft combo with 6–8 lb line is even more fun.

Bait: live shiners are the #1 Florida bass bait (fished under a bobber), red worms and crickets for bluegill, small minnows and little jigs for crappie, plastic worms and spinnerbaits when you want lures.

The bobber rig: hook, small split shot, bobber clipped a foot or two above the hook. Cast it near weeds, docks or fallen trees and watch the bobber — when it goes under, reel.

Tap a knot to jump to its picture and the steps.

Knot 1 · To a hook

Palomar Knot — the easy strong one

Best for: hooks and lures — the strongest easy knot there is.

1. Fold the line in half so you have a loop.
2. Push the loop through the hook's eye.
3. Tie a simple overhand knot with the loop — don't pull it tight yet.
4. Drop the hook through the loop, wet the line, pull tight. Snip the extra.
Knot 2 · To a hook

Improved Clinch Knot — the classic

Best for: hooks and swivels — the classic everyone learns first.

1. Push the line through the hook's eye, leaving a hand-length of extra line.
2. Wrap the extra end around the main line 5 times.
3. Push the end through the little loop by the eye, then through the big loop you just made.
4. Wet it, pull tight slowly, snip the extra.
Knot 3 · To a hook

Uni Knot — the one that does everything

Best for: almost anything — hooks, swivels, even joining two lines.

1. Push the line through the eye and fold it back so the line doubles up.
2. Make a loop with the extra end, held against the doubled line.
3. Wrap the end through the loop and around both lines 4 to 6 times.
4. Wet it, pull the end to snug the wraps, then slide the knot down to the eye and pull the main line tight.
Knot 4 · To a hook

Trilene Knot — through the eye twice

Best for: hooks and swivels — extra strong, because the line goes through the eye twice.

1. Push the line through the hook’s eye TWICE, so it makes a double loop at the eye.
2. Wrap the loose end around the main line 5 to 6 times.
3. Push the end back through the double loop at the eye.
4. Wet it, pull tight slowly, snip the extra.
Knot 5 · To a hook

San Diego Jam — the strong one

Best for: big hooks and heavy lures — one of the strongest hook knots there is.

1. Push the line through the eye and bring the end back so it lies alongside the main line.
2. Wrap the end around BOTH lines 5 to 7 times, working up away from the hook.
3. Push the end through the little gap by the eye, then through the loop the wraps made.
4. Wet it, pull tight slowly, snip the extra.
Knot 6 · To a hook

Snell Knot — made for circle hooks

Best for: circle hooks — it pulls dead straight, so the hook sets in the corner of the mouth like it should.

1. Push the line through the eye and back through it again, leaving a long loop lying along the hook’s shank.
2. Hold the loop against the shank and wrap it around the shank and the line 5 to 7 times, working down the shank.
3. Hold the wraps in place and pull the main line slowly — the wraps snug down tight on the shank.
4. Pull it fully tight and snip the extra.
Knot 7 · For lures

Non-Slip Loop Knot — lets lures swim

Best for: lures — the open loop lets a lure swim and dart like it’s alive.

1. Tie a simple overhand knot in the line a few inches from the end — leave it loose.
2. Push the end through the lure’s eye, then back through the loose overhand knot.
3. Wrap the end around the main line 3 to 4 times.
4. Push the end through the overhand knot one more time, wet it, and pull the main line tight — leave a small loop at the lure.
Knot 8 · Line to line

Double Uni Knot — line to line

Best for: joining two lines — main line to leader, or braid to leader.

1. Lay the two lines side by side, overlapping by about a hand’s length.
2. With one loose end, form a loop and wrap it around BOTH lines 4 to 5 times, then pull that knot snug (not fully tight).
3. Do the exact same knot with the other loose end, facing the other way.
4. Wet both knots, then pull the two main lines apart — the knots slide together and lock. Snip the loose ends close.
Knot 9 · Line to line

Blood Knot — the slim join

Best for: joining two lines of about the same thickness — smooth and slim.

1. Cross the two lines so they overlap by about a hand’s length, like an X.
2. Wrap one loose end around the other line 5 times, then poke it back through the gap in the middle.
3. Wrap the other loose end around the first line 5 times the other way, and poke it through the same middle gap from the opposite side.
4. Wet it and pull both main lines slowly — the wraps stack up neatly. Snip the loose ends close.
Knot 10 · To the reel

Arbor Knot — line to reel

Best for: tying fresh line onto a reel spool so it doesn’t spin.

1. Wrap the line once around the spool and tie a simple overhand knot around the main line. Snip that extra close.
2. Tie a second overhand knot in the loose end itself — it acts as a stopper.
3. Pull the main line so both knots slide down and snug against the spool.
4. Start winding the reel — the line grips the spool and loads up tight.

Sizes run backwards

Hook Sizes, In Plain English

• Size 6–8 — tiny hooks for bluegill and small panfish. Big number = small hook.
• 2/0 circle — the bay all-rounder: trout, redfish, snapper.
• 5/0–7/0 — offshore size for grouper and big snapper.
Once the number gets a "/0" it flips: a 5/0 is BIGGER than a 2/0.

Never fished before? Start at Step 1 and go in order. By Step 12 you have caught a fish.

Step 1 · Before you go

Grown-ups need a license

Kids under 16 fish free — no license. Grown-ups need a Florida freshwater fishing license, and fishing without one can get you a ticket. The license card at the top of this page shows how to get one.
Step 2 · Your rod

Start with a simple spinning combo

A spinning rod and reel that come together in one package. Medium size is right for the lakes around Tampa. Any tackle shop can spool it with line for you. It's the easiest setup there is to learn on.
Step 3 · Tie your hook on

Use the Palomar knot

One knot is all you need to start: the Palomar. It's the strongest easy knot there is.

See the Palomar knot steps →
Step 4 · Add a bobber

Your bite alarm

Clip a bobber onto your line about 2 feet above the hook, with a small split-shot weight pinched on under it. The bobber floats on top. When a fish bites, the bobber goes under. That's your signal.
Step 5 · Bait the hook

Start with a worm

Thread a worm onto the hook so the point just pokes out the other side. Worms are the #1 beginner bait in the lakes — bluegill and bass both love them.
Step 6 · Cast it out

Smooth, not hard

Point the rod where you want the bait to land. Swing it forward in one smooth motion and let the line fly off. Whipping it hard just tangles it. Smooth beats strong.
Step 7 · Wait and watch

Watch the bobber

Let the bait sit. Little bounces mean little fish are nibbling. When the bobber goes all the way under and stays under — that's a real bite. Get ready.
Step 8 · Set the hook

One quick snap

Lift the rod tip up fast — one quick snap of the wrist. That sticks the hook in. Firm, not violent: you're setting a hook, not starting a lawn mower.
Step 9 · Reel it in

Steady wins

Reel in steady and keep the rod tip up. If the fish pulls hard, stop reeling and let it pull for a second, then reel again. Never yank — yanking tears the hook out.
Step 10 · Land your fish

You caught one

Slide a net under the fish, or lift it in one smooth motion. Wet your hands before you touch it — dry hands rub off the slime that protects the fish's skin.
Step 11 · Take the hook out

Pliers make it easy

Hold the fish gently and push the hook out backwards with pliers — the way it went in. If a fish swallowed the hook deep and you're letting it go, cut the line close to the hook instead. The hook rusts away.
Step 12 · Keep it or let it go

Check the size first

Keeping it? Measure it and check the Fish Chart — a fish that's too small has to go back, it's the law. Keepers go straight on ice. Letting it go? Hold it in the water, move it slowly back and forth, and let it swim out of your hands on its own.

You caught it — now make it dinner. Go slow. A sharp knife does the work for you.

First · The law

Size-limit fish stay whole until you’re home

A fish with a size limit has to stay whole — head, tail and all — until you're back on land. Filleting one on the boat or at the ramp can get you a ticket. Fillet at home.
You need

Your tools

A sharp fillet knife, a cutting board, pliers, a bowl of clean water, zip bags and a cooler of ice. A dull knife means hard work and cut fingers — sharp is safer.
Step 1

Ice it right away

The moment you decide to keep a fish, it goes on ice. A cold fish fillets cleaner and tastes ten times better.
Step 2

Rinse it and lay it flat

Rinse the slime off. Lay the fish flat on the board — right-handed, head pointing left. Keep your holding hand curled, fingers tucked back out of the knife's path.
Step 3

First cut — behind the gill

Cut down right behind the gill plate until the knife touches the backbone. Stop there — don't cut through the backbone.
Step 4

Slide along the backbone

Turn the knife flat, pointing toward the tail. Slide it along the top of the backbone all the way to the tail in long, smooth strokes. Let the bones steer the blade.
Step 5

Take the meat off the skin

Lay the fillet skin-side down. Grab the tail end of the skin with pliers. Hold the skin tight and slide the knife flat between the skin and the meat, cutting away from you.
Step 6

Trim and check for bones

Cut out the dark red strip down the center — that's the strong, fishy taste. Run your fingertips over the fillet: any little bones you feel, pull them out with pliers.
Step 7

Rinse, bag, ice

Rinse the fillet quick, pat it dry, and seal it in a bag. Bury the bag in ice. Eat it within 2 days — or freeze it the day you catch it.
Whole fish instead?

Cleaning a fish whole

Cut the belly open from the tail to the gills and pull the guts out. Cooking it with the skin on? Scrape the scales off with the back of a spoon, tail to head. Rinse inside and out, then ice it.

About Tampa Bay Boating

Planning a day on the water used to mean a dozen different websites and apps — one for the tides, one for the forecast, one for ramps and fuel. We got tired of it, so we built TampaBayBoating.com: everything boating in Tampa Bay, in one place.

We're David and Nancy — two avid boaters.

Boating is expensive enough — the boat, the fuel, the repairs. This website was never going to be one more cost. TampaBayBoating.com is 100% free to use for everyone, and it stays that way: local marine businesses support it so boaters never pay.

Here's what's on it:

  • Live tides & marine forecast — know before you go
  • Daily fishing reports — what's biting and where
  • Boater's Map — ramps, marinas, fuel docks, bait shops and more, all on one map
  • Cheapest fuel finder — we check non-ethanol fuel prices every morning
  • Events calendar — everything happening on the water
  • Dock & Dine — waterfront restaurants you can pull your boat up to

On the Radio

The channels every Tampa Bay boater should know — and how to get bridges opened.

Know your channels

Key VHF Channels

• Ch 16 — Distress, safety & hailing. Monitor it whenever you're underway
• Ch 9 — Bridge tenders & secondary calling (use this to hail drawbridges)
• Ch 13 — Commercial vessel traffic ("bridge-to-bridge")
• Ch 22A — U.S. Coast Guard working channel (after first contact on 16)
• Ch 68 / 69 / 71 / 72 — Recreational working channels
• WX 1–7 — NOAA weather broadcasts, 24/7
How to hail a bridge

Asking for an Opening

Switch to VHF Ch 9, then say the bridge name three times followed by your vessel name:

"Alafia Railroad Bridge, Alafia Railroad Bridge, Alafia Railroad Bridge — this is Sea Breeze."

Tell them your direction of travel and ask for their next opening. Approach slow, go through at no-wake speed once it's fully open. Most bay bridges (Skyway, Gandy, Howard Frankland, Courtney Campbell) are fixed high spans — no opening needed.
Alafia River

Alafia River Railroad Bridge (CSX)

The train bridge near the mouth of the Alafia. Hail on VHF Ch 9 — the bridge operates on signal per federal regulations.
Hillsborough River, Tampa

Downtown Drawbridges

Platt St, Brorein St, Kennedy Blvd, Cass St, Laurel St, Columbus Dr & Hillsborough Ave — open on signal with at least 2 hours notice (federal reg 33 CFR 117.291). Hail on VHF Ch 9. The CSX railroad bridge (mile 0.7) is normally kept fully open.

Park It Right

Dry stack, wet slips, and land lots — where to keep your boat when you're not on it.

Advice

Which Storage Is Right for You?

• Dry stack: forklift puts your boat on a rack indoors. Best hurricane protection, no bottom paint needed, call-ahead launch. Usually 20–30 ft boats.
• Wet slip: your boat lives in the water at a marina. Most convenient — walk on and go — but needs bottom paint, zincs, and a hurricane plan.
• Land lot: park the boat on its trailer in a gated lot. Cheapest option, great if you trailer to different ramps. Check gate hours and whether wash-down water is available.
• Ask every facility: hurricane policy, insurance requirements, access hours, and waitlists — the good ones fill up before storm season.

Under Safety

Read the water like a local — markers, charts, and the rules that keep everyone safe.

Markers

Reading Channel Markers

• "Red Right Returning" — coming IN from the Gulf (returning), keep red markers on your right (starboard), green on your left
• Heading OUT to the Gulf, it's reversed: green on your right
• Markers are numbered — numbers increase as you head inland
• Yellow marks = Intracoastal Waterway — follow them down the ICW
• If you're outside the markers, you're outside the channel — Tampa Bay shoals fast
Charts

Reading a Nautical Chart

• Numbers = water depth in feet at low tide — know your draft and add margin
• Blue shading = shallow, white = deeper water
• Magenta lines mark dredged channels — stay inside them in unfamiliar water
• Symbols show rocks, wrecks, and obstructions — learn the dozen most common ones
• Free charts: NOAA Office of Coast Survey at nauticalcharts.noaa.gov — paper and digital
Tampa Bay & surrounding area

Get the Local Charts

• On this page: the boater's map now has a nautical chart layer — toggle it on to see real buoys, beacons, and lights plotted on Tampa Bay
• NOAA Chart 11412 — Tampa Bay and St. Joseph Sound, 1:80,000. NOAA retired the old paper raster charts in 2024; today's charts are built from live electronic data
• Free & current: NOAA's Custom Chart tool builds a printable, up-to-date chart of any area from live data — search "NOAA Custom Chart"
• Paper: print-on-demand dealers print 11412 corrected to the latest Notice to Mariners — example listing
Right of way

Rules of the Road

• Pass port-to-port — like cars, keep the other boat on your left when meeting head-on
• Crossing: the vessel on your right has the right of way — you slow down or turn behind them
• Overtaking: the boat passing keeps clear of the boat being passed
• A sailboat under power counts as a powerboat
• Big ships can't stop or turn — stay out of the shipping channel and never cross a ship's bow
• Full rules: U.S. Coast Guard Navigation Rules at navcen.uscg.gov
Tampa Bay

Local Knowledge

• The bay averages only ~12 feet deep — and much of it is far shallower. Idle out until you know the water
• Winter low tides + north wind can drop the bay a foot or more — flats you ran in summer may be dry
• The shipping channel (Port Tampa to the Gulf) is for big traffic — cross it quickly, at right angles, never linger
• Skyway area: strong currents around the bridge pilings and the fishing piers — plan your drift
• When in doubt: slow down, follow the markers, and ask a local

The best education is hands-on — consider a local on-water course or a trip with a licensed captain.

Boat Smarts

Everything they don't teach you at the dealership — launching, anchoring, docking, knots, and keeping her running.

The ramp

Launching Like a Pro

• Prep BEFORE the ramp — drain plug IN, transom straps off, gear loaded, lines & fenders ready. The ramp is for launching, not packing
• Don't block the ramp — launch, move the boat to the courtesy dock, then park the truck
• Back down slow and straight; have your crew in position before the boat floats
• At pickup, load the boat, pull fully clear of the ramp before strapping down
Staying put

Anchoring

• Scope 7:1 — let out 7 feet of rode for every foot of water depth (5:1 minimum in calm weather)
• Always anchor from the bow — never the stern (a wake over the transom swamps boats)
• Back down slowly on the anchor to set it, then check your position against a landmark
• Danforth/fluke anchors are king in Tampa Bay sand and mud
• Leave swing room — you and every boat around you will swing with wind and tide
Lines

Knots & Tying Up

• Learn three knots and you're set: cleat hitch (dock lines), bowline (loop that won't jam), clove hitch (quick tie to a piling)
• On a cleat: around, cross, cross, tuck — never just wrap it in circles
• Use spring lines (bow line led aft, stern line led forward) to stop fore-and-aft surging
• Leave slack for the tide — Tampa Bay swings ~2 feet; tight lines at low tide become guitar strings at high
The dock

Docking

• Slow is pro — approach at the speed you're willing to hit the dock with
• Fenders out BEFORE you enter the marina, on the docking side
• Judge wind and current first — they matter more than your steering
• Get a spring line on first, then the boat can't go anywhere while you sort the rest
• Brief your crew: who handles which line, before you're in the slip. Never jump — step
Fuel dock

Fueling Up

• No smoking, engines off, everyone off the boat except the person fueling
• Know your tank size — don't overfill; fuel expands in the Florida heat
• Run the blower 4+ minutes before starting inboards after fueling
• Wipe up every spill — fuel in the water is a fine and a jerk move

Know Before You Go

What to expect, what they'll check, and what you need aboard. Stay calm — most stops end with a wave.

In the moment

What To Do

• Slow down and stop — idle down, keep hands visible
• Don't reach suddenly — their first question is often "any weapons aboard?" Answer without reaching for anything
• Be polite and brief — they're doing a job; attitude turns a check into a citation
• Don't argue on the water — if you disagree with a citation, fight it later, not on the boat
• Have documents somewhere you can get to without digging through compartments
New Florida law

The Boater Freedom Act

Florida's Boater Freedom Act (SB 1388) was signed May 19, 2025 and took effect July 1, 2025. Here's what it means when you see blue lights:

• FWC and local officers now need probable cause to stop or board your vessel — they can no longer pull you over solely for a random safety or sanitation equipment check
• Safety equipment violations are now secondary offenses — they can't be the reason for the stop, only something found during a lawful one
• The "Florida Freedom Boater" decal — issued at registration/renewal after you show your safety gear is compliant — signals your boat meets requirements
• The law also blocks local governments from banning gas-powered boats or restricting watercraft by fuel type

What it does NOT change:
• The U.S. Coast Guard can still board any vessel, any time — no warrant, no probable cause needed (federal law, 14 USC §522, upheld in United States v. Villamonte-Marquez, 462 U.S. 579 (1983))
• Probable-cause stops still happen — e.g. fishing gear in plain view with no registration displayed, an observed violation, or BUI suspicion
• Manatee zones, seagrass protections, BUI laws (0.08% / 0.02% under 21), and fishing regulations are all still fully enforced
The inspection

What They'll Check

• Registration — current, aboard the boat; hull numbers 3"+ and matching
• Safety gear — PFDs for everyone, throwable, extinguisher, signals, horn/whistle
• Your cooler & livewell — if you've been fishing, FWC can open and inspect coolers and fish boxes for size/bag compliance
• Fishing licenses — everyone fishing needs one
• BUI — 0.08% BAC limit on the water (0.02% if under 21). The operator is the one tested.
Be ready

Have These Aboard

• Vessel registration (the actual document)
• Photo ID for the operator
• Fishing licenses for everyone fishing
• All required safety equipment — accessible, not buried
• A measuring device and a copy of current size/bag limits

Pro tip: keep documents in a waterproof pouch in the console. The faster the check goes, the faster you're back to fishing.

Slow Down

Tampa Bay's speed zones change with the calendar — here's how to stay legal and keep the sea cows safe.

Seasonal

Zones That Change

• Apr 1 – Nov 15: Slow speed in Old Tampa Bay north of the Courtney Campbell Causeway, plus Safety Harbor and parts of Old Tampa Bay (Pinellas side)

Always Slow

Storm Season

June through November. Have a plan before the cone points at Tampa Bay.

Decide early

Haul Out or Stay In?

• Hauling out is the safest option — book your haul-out plan with a yard before hurricane season; slots vanish when a storm forms

Do You Need It?

Don't Be That Boater

The unwritten rules of Tampa Bay — how to launch without holding up the whole ramp, ride on someone else's boat without wearing out your welcome, and share the sandbar like a local.

Boat Ramp

The Ramp Is a Loading Zone

• Prep in the staging area, not on the ramp. Gear loaded, drain plug in, transom straps off, lines and fenders ready — all before your trailer touches the water
• On the ramp it's three moves: back down, float the boat off, clear the lane. That's it
• Walk the boat to the courtesy dock, then go park the truck. Never rig rods, load coolers, or sunscreen the kids while your trailer sits in the lane
• Coming back in, same rule in reverse: load the boat, pull up to the lot, THEN wipe down, strap up, and sort the gear
• At night ramps, kill your headlights while others back down — your lights blind the driver next to you
• Somebody struggling? Offer a hand. Everyone in line has fought a crosswind at a busy ramp
Guest Rules

Get Invited Back

• Offer to pay for fuel, bait, or ice — and actually hand it over. A day on the water costs the owner real money, even when you catch nothing
• Never invite anyone along — friends, kids, dogs — without asking the captain first
• Show up on time, in soft non-marking shoes, with your own sunscreen, drinks, and a towel
• Ask before bringing glass — plenty of captains ban bottles outright
• Don't touch the helm, throttle, or electronics unless you're handed them
• Pitch in without being asked: dock lines coming in, hauling the anchor, and scrubbing the boat down at the end of the day
• The captain's call is final — when to leave, where to fish, and when the weather says head in
Underway

Your Wake, Your Problem

• You're responsible for your wake. If it slams a docked boat or rocks a kayak, that's on you — legally and otherwise
• Slow down and pass wide around anchored boats, kayaks, paddleboards, and anyone fishing a shoreline
• Never anchor in a channel, in front of a ramp, or blocking a dock entrance
• Divers-down flag up? Florida law: idle speed within 300 feet in open water, 100 feet in channels and inlets
• If another boat is already fishing a spot, it's theirs. Set up somewhere else
Sandbar

Share the Sandbar

• Anchor with room to swing — don't park on top of the boat next to you, and don't raft up to strangers uninvited
• Keep the music at your boat. If the family two boats down can hear every word, it's too loud
• Whatever you bring leaves with you. Sunk cans and bottles become somebody's cut foot or bent prop
• Bathroom breaks happen at the boat's head or out in deeper water — not in the shallows where kids are wading
Fuel Dock & Marina

In and Out

• Lines and fenders ready BEFORE you pull in — the attendant shouldn't wait while you dig them out of a hatch
• Fuel up, pay, and move off. The fuel dock is not parking
• No smoking or open flame anywhere near the pumps — ever
• Tied up at the dock? Coil your lines and keep the walkway clear for everyone else
