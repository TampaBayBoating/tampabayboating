New to Boating?

Everything you need to get on the water safely — in the right order. No experience assumed.

1Get legal first

Born on or after Jan 1, 1988? Florida requires a Boating Safety Education ID card to operate a boat with 10 HP or more. Take an approved course and carry the card.

→ 2Gear up: the safety checklist

Life jackets for everyone, throwable cushion, fire extinguisher, flares, horn, kill-switch lanyard. See exactly what the Coast Guard requires.

→ 3Rent before you buy

Try different boats through rentals or a boat club before spending a dollar. Learn what you actually like on the water first.

→ 4Learn the markers

“Red, right, returning” — channel markers, no-wake signs, and who has the right of way. The rules that keep everyone safe.

→ 5Launch, dock & anchor

Ramp etiquette, docking without the drama, knots that hold, and anchoring that actually sticks.

→ 6Respect Tampa Bay

Skinny water outside the channels, strong tidal current, afternoon lightning, and slow-speed manatee zones. Local knowledge that saves props and tickets.

→ 7Before every trip

Check the marine forecast, tell someone your float plan, and monitor VHF channel 16. If the bay looks angry, stay at the dock.

Boat Smart

Boat Maintenance

The routine that keeps you off the towboat's hook.

1After every trip

Flush the engine with fresh water for 5 minutes after every saltwater run — it saves thousands. Wash the boat and trailer, check the bilge, wipe down vinyl before the sun bakes the salt in.

2Monthly

Check battery charge and clean terminals — a dead battery strands more boaters than anything else. Add fuel stabilizer if she sits more than a few weeks. Check safety gear expiry dates, inspect dock lines and fenders.

3Yearly

Full engine service, replace sacrificial anodes, inspect trailer bearings and tires, check fire extinguishers, and refresh bottom paint if she lives in a slip.

4Florida extras

Salt corrosion never sleeps here — rinse religiously. Have a hurricane haul-out plan ready before June. And cover the boat: the Florida sun destroys vinyl and gelcoat fast.

The 5-minute rule

If you only do one thing, flush the engine after every saltwater trip. It is the cheapest insurance in boating.

Tampa Bay Fishing Report

What's biting right now — plus the size and bag limits you need to stay legal.

Daily Report — Monday, October 12, 2026

Today's Best Bets

1. Redfish — Red October is on. South Shore flats from Cockroach Bay to Joe Bay are the hot ticket, with schools of ~50 reds tailing and working the mangrove edges on higher water — and oversized fish in the mix, with cut ladyfish taking some big ones near the Howard Frankland. Live pinfish (2–4″) under the bushes or live whitebait — fish the incoming 10:13 AM–4:35 PM as water floods the flats.
2. Snook — the fall shift continues: reports have them moving off the beaches and passes into the bay, stacking around docks, bars and mangrove points, with most running smaller ahead of winter. Free-line live sardines on moving water — the last of the morning outgoing (through 10:13 AM), then the turn to incoming midday, and again on the evening outgoing after 4:35 PM.
3. Trout — the bigger ones keep moving shallow. Topwater at sunrise on south bay flats like Pinellas Point and the Clam Bar east of the Skyway, then jigs (MirrOlure Marsh Minnow Jr. on a ¼ oz head) worked slow along grass edges as the incoming tide fills in from 10:13 AM.
Note: flounder season closes Oct 15 — three days left to keep one (min 14″, 5/day). Target sandy patches and sandbars on the grass flats with shrimp or jigs bounced on the bottom.

Tide windows: low 10:13 AM, high 4:35 PM — incoming 10:15 AM–4:35 PM on the flats, outgoing 4:35–9:19 PM around pilings, passes and channel edges.
Bait: live pinfish (redfish candy) + live sardines (snook killer). Find a bait shop near the ramp · Check today's tides & forecast.

Don't forget

Licenses & Seasons

• FL saltwater fishing license required — get one at GoOutdoorsFlorida.com
• Snook season (Gulf): open Mar–Apr & Sep–Nov; closed Dec–Feb & May–Aug
• Measure fish pinched tail, lying flat — when in doubt, let it go

Timing

⏰ When to Go

• Fish the moving tide — incoming and outgoing beat slack water every time. Check today's tides above
• Dawn & dusk — low light means topwater plugs and aggressive reds, snook & trout
• Night dock lights — snook and trout stack up under lit docks; quiet approach, live shrimp
• Summer: go early — bite dies by mid-morning heat. Winter: wait for the afternoon warm-up
• After a front — falling barometer ahead of storms often triggers a hot bite
Tampa Bay area

Where to Try

• Weedon Island flats — redfish & trout on the grass flats
• Skyway bridge pilings — mangrove snapper, grouper, tarpon (seasonal)
• Fort De Soto — beaches, piers & backwater; great for families
• Egmont Key — run offshore of the island for kings, cobia & tarpon
• Alafia River mouth — snook & reds around the mangroves
• Upper bay creeks — trout & flounder in the potholes
Beyond the bay

Heading Offshore

• Nearshore reefs & ledges (3–9 mi) — king mackerel, Spanish, cobia & tripletail around structure; great on calm days
• Hard bottom & ledges (60–120 ft) — red grouper, mangrove snapper & lane snapper on live pinfish or cut bait
• Deeper drops (120 ft+) — gag grouper, red snapper (seasonal) & amberjack; anchor up-current and chum

Before you go:
• Check the marine forecast — see today's conditions. If seas are 3–5 ft, stay home
• File a float plan — tell someone where you're going and when you'll be back
• 1/3 fuel rule — 1/3 out, 1/3 back, 1/3 reserve. No exceptions
• VHF radio + EPIRB/PLB — cell signal dies fast offshore. See the safety section
• Federal waters start at 9 nm — different bag limits & seasons apply beyond the line. Verify with FWC before keeping fish
Tackle box

What to Throw

• Live shrimp — the universal Tampa Bay bait; everything eats it
• Greenbacks (whitebait) — the local candy for snook, trout & reds
• Topwater plugs — dawn patrol over the flats
• Soft plastics on jig heads — paddle tails in new penny & white
• Cut ladyfish or mullet — for bull reds

Paddle Up

Launch areas, mangrove tunnels, and paddling safety around Tampa Bay.

Stay safe

Paddling Safety

• Wear your PFD — Florida requires one aboard; wearing it is what saves lives
• Carry a whistle and a white light if you're out near dark
• Stay out of marked channels — you're invisible to fast boats; cross quickly at right angles
• Check wind before you go — a 15-knot bay breeze against you is a long paddle home
• Tell someone your float plan: where you're launching and when you'll be back
• Hydrate + sun protection — the bay reflects sun from every direction
No gear?

Rentals & Lessons

• Kayak, SUP, and canoe rentals are available at Weedon Island and Honeymoon Island
• Local outfitters around the bay offer guided mangrove tunnel tours — great first trip
• Many bait & tackle shops know who rents nearby — ask when you grab bait
• Try before you buy: renting a few times tells you whether you want a sit-on-top, touring kayak, or SUP
Pro tip

Tides Matter

• Mangrove tunnels need water — go at mid to high tide or you'll be dragging
• Check today's tides before heading to Weedon Island or Cockroach Bay
• Early morning = calmest water, best wildlife, fewer boats
• Incoming tide pushes you in, outgoing pulls you out — plan the easy direction home

On the Radio

The channels every Tampa Bay boater should know — and how to get bridges opened.

Know your channels

Key VHF Channels

• Ch 16 — Distress, safety & hailing. Monitor it whenever you're underway
• Ch 9 — Bridge tenders & secondary calling (use this to hail drawbridges)
• Ch 13 — Commercial vessel traffic ("bridge-to-bridge")
• Ch 22A — U.S. Coast Guard working channel (after first contact on 16)
• Ch 68 / 69 / 71 / 72 — Recreational working channels
• WX 1–7 — NOAA weather broadcasts, 24/7
How to hail a bridge

Asking for an Opening

Switch to VHF Ch 9, then say the bridge name three times followed by your vessel name:

"Alafia Railroad Bridge, Alafia Railroad Bridge, Alafia Railroad Bridge — this is Sea Breeze."

Tell them your direction of travel and ask for their next opening. Approach slow, go through at no-wake speed once it's fully open. Most bay bridges (Skyway, Gandy, Howard Frankland, Courtney Campbell) are fixed high spans — no opening needed.
Alafia River

Alafia River Railroad Bridge (CSX)

The train bridge near the mouth of the Alafia. Hail on VHF Ch 9 — the bridge operates on signal per federal regulations.
Hillsborough River, Tampa

Downtown Drawbridges

Platt St, Brorein St, Kennedy Blvd, Cass St, Laurel St, Columbus Dr & Hillsborough Ave — open on signal with at least 2 hours notice (federal reg 33 CFR 117.291). Hail on VHF Ch 9. The CSX railroad bridge (mile 0.7) is normally kept fully open.
Broke down?

On-Water Towing

True emergency (sinking, fire, medical): VHF Ch 16 or call 911 — reach the Coast Guard first.

Just need a tow (dead engine, ran aground, out of fuel) — hail on VHF Ch 16 or call:
• Sea Tow Tampa Bay — 24/7, (727) 547-1868
• TowBoatUS Tampa Bay — 24-hr dispatch (800) 391-4869

A membership (~$165–$249/yr) covers unlimited towing — one tow without it can run $600+. Worth every penny.
Sea Tow Tampa Bay · TowBoatUS Tampa Bay

Bridge schedules and tender numbers can change — confirm on VHF Ch 9 before you go.

Park It Right

Dry stack, wet slips, and land lots — where to keep your boat when you're not on it.

Advice

Which Storage Is Right for You?

• Dry stack: forklift puts your boat on a rack indoors. Best hurricane protection, no bottom paint needed, call-ahead launch. Usually 20–30 ft boats.
• Wet slip: your boat lives in the water at a marina. Most convenient — walk on and go — but needs bottom paint, zincs, and a hurricane plan.
• Land lot: park the boat on its trailer in a gated lot. Cheapest option, great if you trailer to different ramps. Check gate hours and whether wash-down water is available.
• Ask every facility: hurricane policy, insurance requirements, access hours, and waitlists — the good ones fill up before storm season.

Call ahead for current rates and availability — storage fills fast before hurricane season. Also plotted on the boater's map under Storage.

Boat Without Owning

All of the boating, none of the boat payment, insurance, storage, or maintenance. Here's how the club model works and who's who around the bay.

How it works

The Boat Club Model

• Pay a one-time entry fee + monthly dues — then reserve boats online, show up, and go
• The club handles fuel, cleaning, maintenance, insurance, and storage
• Most clubs cap you at ~4 rolling reservations so no one hogs the fleet
• Boats typically back at the dock by sunset — this is daytime boating
• Many memberships include reciprocal access at other locations or even nationwide
Is it worth it?

Club vs. Owning

• One analysis put 5 years of club membership at ~$30–35K all-in vs. $90–140K to own a comparable 22-ft center console kept in a marina
• Owning wins if you have a dock behind your house, go 80+ days a year, or run offshore regularly
• The club wins for weekend warriors who want variety — fish one day, tube the next, sail the next
• Try before you buy: a year in a club teaches you exactly what boat you'd actually want to own

Under Safety

Read the water like a local — markers, charts, and the rules that keep everyone safe.

Markers

Reading Channel Markers

• "Red Right Returning" — coming IN from the Gulf (returning), keep red markers on your right (starboard), green on your left
• Heading OUT to the Gulf, it's reversed: green on your right
• Markers are numbered — numbers increase as you head inland
• Yellow marks = Intracoastal Waterway — follow them down the ICW
• If you're outside the markers, you're outside the channel — Tampa Bay shoals fast
Charts

Reading a Nautical Chart

• Numbers = water depth in feet at low tide — know your draft and add margin
• Blue shading = shallow, white = deeper water
• Magenta lines mark dredged channels — stay inside them in unfamiliar water
• Symbols show rocks, wrecks, and obstructions — learn the dozen most common ones
• Free charts: NOAA Office of Coast Survey at nauticalcharts.noaa.gov — paper and digital
Tampa Bay & surrounding area

Get the Local Charts

• On this page: the boater's map now has a nautical chart layer — toggle it on to see real buoys, beacons, and lights plotted on Tampa Bay
• NOAA Chart 11412 — Tampa Bay and St. Joseph Sound, 1:80,000. NOAA retired the old paper raster charts in 2024; today's charts are built from live electronic data
• Free & current: NOAA's Custom Chart tool builds a printable, up-to-date chart of any area from live data — search "NOAA Custom Chart"
• Paper: print-on-demand dealers print 11412 corrected to the latest Notice to Mariners — example listing
Right of way

Rules of the Road

• Pass port-to-port — like cars, keep the other boat on your left when meeting head-on
• Crossing: the vessel on your right has the right of way — you slow down or turn behind them
• Overtaking: the boat passing keeps clear of the boat being passed
• A sailboat under power counts as a powerboat
• Big ships can't stop or turn — stay out of the shipping channel and never cross a ship's bow
• Full rules: U.S. Coast Guard Navigation Rules at navcen.uscg.gov
Tampa Bay

Local Knowledge

• The bay averages only ~12 feet deep — and much of it is far shallower. Idle out until you know the water
• Winter low tides + north wind can drop the bay a foot or more — flats you ran in summer may be dry
• The shipping channel (Port Tampa to the Gulf) is for big traffic — cross it quickly, at right angles, never linger
• Skyway area: strong currents around the bridge pilings and the fishing piers — plan your drift
• When in doubt: slow down, follow the markers, and ask a local

The best education is hands-on — consider a local on-water course or a trip with a licensed captain.

Boat Smarts

Everything they don't teach you at the dealership — launching, anchoring, docking, knots, and keeping her running.

The ramp

Launching Like a Pro

• Prep BEFORE the ramp — drain plug IN, transom straps off, gear loaded, lines & fenders ready. The ramp is for launching, not packing
• Don't block the ramp — launch, move the boat to the courtesy dock, then park the truck
• Back down slow and straight; have your crew in position before the boat floats
• At pickup, load the boat, pull fully clear of the ramp before strapping down
Staying put

Anchoring

• Scope 7:1 — let out 7 feet of rode for every foot of water depth (5:1 minimum in calm weather)
• Always anchor from the bow — never the stern (a wake over the transom swamps boats)
• Back down slowly on the anchor to set it, then check your position against a landmark
• Danforth/fluke anchors are king in Tampa Bay sand and mud
• Leave swing room — you and every boat around you will swing with wind and tide
Lines

Knots & Tying Up

• Learn three knots and you're set: cleat hitch (dock lines), bowline (loop that won't jam), clove hitch (quick tie to a piling)
• On a cleat: around, cross, cross, tuck — never just wrap it in circles
• Use spring lines (bow line led aft, stern line led forward) to stop fore-and-aft surging
• Leave slack for the tide — Tampa Bay swings ~2 feet; tight lines at low tide become guitar strings at high
The dock

Docking

• Slow is pro — approach at the speed you're willing to hit the dock with
• Fenders out BEFORE you enter the marina, on the docking side
• Judge wind and current first — they matter more than your steering
• Get a spring line on first, then the boat can't go anywhere while you sort the rest
• Brief your crew: who handles which line, before you're in the slip. Never jump — step
Fuel dock

Fueling Up

• No smoking, engines off, everyone off the boat except the person fueling
• Know your tank size — don't overfill; fuel expands in the Florida heat
• Run the blower 4+ minutes before starting inboards after fueling
• Wipe up every spill — fuel in the water is a fine and a jerk move

Boat Smart

What the U.S. Coast Guard requires on your boat — plus Florida rules and smart habits for Tampa Bay waters.

Federal law

Required by the U.S. Coast Guard

• One USCG-approved life jacket per person — right size, accessible, in good condition
• Kids under 13 must wear one while underway
• Throwable flotation device (boats 16 ft and longer)
• Fire extinguisher, marine-rated — charged and not expired (12-year max)
• Sound device — horn or whistle (40 ft+ also needs a bell)
• Visual distress signals — day + night on coastal waters
• Navigation lights from sunset to sunrise
• Kill-switch lanyard attached (boats under 26 ft)
• Registration numbers displayed and papers aboard
USCG Boating Safety Center
Florida

Florida Rules to Know

• Born on/after Jan 1, 1988? You need a Boating Safety Education ID card to operate 10 HP+
• Kids under 6 must wear a life jacket while underway (vessels under 26 ft)
• BUI = DUI — 0.08 limit, same as the road
• Fly a divers-down flag when anyone's snorkeling or diving
• Obey no-wake and manatee zones — they're everywhere in the bay
FWC Boating Regulations
Before you go

Leave-the-Dock Checklist

• Check the forecast — see today's tides & marine weather
• File a float plan — tell someone where you're going and when you'll be back
• Fuel rule: ⅓ out, ⅓ back, ⅓ in reserve
• Walk through the USCG checklist above — every trip
• Charge phones, bring water, sun protection
On the water

Smart Habits

• Wear the life jacket — the best one is the one you'll actually put on
• Kill switch on, every single trip
• Watch the sky — Tampa Bay storms build fast; head in early
• Engine off when swimmers are near the boat
• Never BUI — designate a sober skipper

Know Before You Go

What to expect, what they'll check, and what you need aboard. Stay calm — most stops end with a wave.

In the moment

What To Do

• Slow down and stop — idle down, keep hands visible
• Don't reach suddenly — their first question is often "any weapons aboard?" Answer without reaching for anything
• Be polite and brief — they're doing a job; attitude turns a check into a citation
• Don't argue on the water — if you disagree with a citation, fight it later, not on the boat
• Have documents somewhere you can get to without digging through compartments
New Florida law

The Boater Freedom Act

Florida's Boater Freedom Act (SB 1388) was signed May 19, 2025 and took effect July 1, 2025. Here's what it means when you see blue lights:

• FWC and local officers now need probable cause to stop or board your vessel — they can no longer pull you over solely for a random safety or sanitation equipment check
• Safety equipment violations are now secondary offenses — they can't be the reason for the stop, only something found during a lawful one
• The "Florida Freedom Boater" decal — issued at registration/renewal after you show your safety gear is compliant — signals your boat meets requirements
• The law also blocks local governments from banning gas-powered boats or restricting watercraft by fuel type

What it does NOT change:
• The U.S. Coast Guard can still board any vessel, any time — no warrant, no probable cause needed (federal law, 14 USC §522, upheld in United States v. Villamonte-Marquez, 462 U.S. 579 (1983))
• Probable-cause stops still happen — e.g. fishing gear in plain view with no registration displayed, an observed violation, or BUI suspicion
• Manatee zones, seagrass protections, BUI laws (0.08% / 0.02% under 21), and fishing regulations are all still fully enforced
The inspection

What They'll Check

• Registration — current, aboard the boat; hull numbers 3"+ and matching
• Safety gear — PFDs for everyone, throwable, extinguisher, signals, horn/whistle
• Your cooler & livewell — if you've been fishing, FWC can open and inspect coolers and fish boxes for size/bag compliance
• Fishing licenses — everyone fishing needs one
• BUI — 0.08% BAC limit on the water (0.02% if under 21). The operator is the one tested.
Be ready

Have These Aboard

• Vessel registration (the actual document)
• Photo ID for the operator
• Fishing licenses for everyone fishing
• All required safety equipment — accessible, not buried
• A measuring device and a copy of current size/bag limits

Pro tip: keep documents in a waterproof pouch in the console. The faster the check goes, the faster you're back to fishing.

Slow Down

Tampa Bay's speed zones change with the calendar — here's how to stay legal and keep the sea cows safe.

Seasonal

Zones That Change

• Apr 1 – Nov 15: Slow speed in Old Tampa Bay north of the Courtney Campbell Causeway, plus Safety Harbor and parts of Old Tampa Bay (Pinellas side)
• Nov 15 – Mar 31: Idle speed in federal manatee refuges (warm-water outflows like power plant discharges) — manatees crowd in for winter warmth
• Seasonal zones are posted with dates — read the whole sign, not just the speed line
Year-round

Always Slow

• Downtown Tampa & Davis Islands channels — narrow, busy, idle speed, heavy enforcement
• Bayshore Blvd seawall and developed shorelines — slow near shore
• Rivers and narrow creeks — assume the zone starts earlier than you think
• Warm-water outflows and preserves (Cockroach Bay, Terra Ceia) — many double as manatee refuges
Good to know

Manatee Basics

• Manatees can't get out of the way of fast boats in time — slow speed saves lives
• Watch for swirls, snouts, and backs breaking the surface, especially in shallow warm water
• A manatee strike can kill the animal and wreck your lower unit — nobody wins
• Report injured manatees: FWC Wildlife Alert 1-888-404-3922
• Zone maps: myfwc.com/manatee — check before exploring new water

Get Legal

Florida law requires it for most operators — here's the rule and where to get certified.

The law

Who Needs It

• Anyone born on or after January 1, 1988 operating a motorboat of 10 HP or more must carry a Florida Boating Safety Education ID Card
• The card never expires — one and done
• You must have it aboard while operating — a photo on your phone works
• Exemptions: private ponds, or operating under direct supervision of an exempt qualified person
Get certified

Where To Take It

• FWC-approved online courses — several are free; search "FWC Boating Safety Education" at myfwc.com for the official provider list
• BoatUS Foundation — free online course, widely used
• America's Boating Club (USPS) — classroom and online options around Tampa Bay
• Visitors renting boats can use a temporary 90-day certificate after passing an approved exam
Worth it

Beyond the Card

• Many insurers give discounts for certified operators — ask yours
• You'll actually learn the navigation rules (who yields to whom) that keep the bay civil
• New boaters: pair the course with a hands-on lesson from a local captain before going solo

Storm Season

June through November. Have a plan before the cone points at Tampa Bay.

Decide early

Haul Out or Stay In?

• Hauling out is the safest option — book your haul-out plan with a yard before hurricane season; slots vanish when a storm forms
• If staying in the water: a well-protected marina with a hurricane plan beats an exposed dock
• Never stay aboard during a hurricane to "ride it out"
• Know your marina's hurricane policy — many require you to leave or move the boat by a set deadline
On the boat

Storm-Ready Checklist

• Strip all canvas — biminis, covers, sails. Wind shreds them and they become sails
• Double all dock lines, add chafe protection, set extra fenders
• Remove valuables, electronics, and documents
• Top off fuel and water, charge batteries, close seacocks
• Photograph the boat's condition for insurance — before and after
After the storm

Recovery

• Don't rush back — wait for official all-clear; channels shift and debris floats
• Watch for submerged objects, missing markers, and shoaling on your first trip out
• Document damage before cleanup for your insurance claim
• Check on your marina neighbors — the boating community looks after its own
• Review what worked and update your plan while it's fresh

Protect It

What to look for in a boat policy — especially in hurricane country.

Why bother

Do You Need It?

• Florida doesn't legally require boat insurance — but most marinas require liability coverage to keep a slip
• Lenders require hull coverage if your boat is financed
• One bad day — a collision, a grounding, a named storm — can cost more than years of premiums
• Umbrella policies on your home/auto often exclude boats — check yours
Coverage

What a Good Policy Has

• Liability — damage/injury you cause to others (marinas usually want $300k+)
• Hull / physical damage — your boat, motor, trailer
• On-water towing — or pair with a Sea Tow / TowBoatUS membership
• Hurricane haul-out coverage — pays to haul your boat when a named storm threatens Florida waters
• Agreed value vs. actual cash value — agreed value pays the insured amount, no depreciation argument
Shop smart

Getting Quotes

• Get quotes from 3+ providers — rates vary wildly for the same boat
• Big names writing boat policies: Progressive, GEICO Marine (BoatUS), State Farm, Allstate — plus specialty marine insurers
• Ask about navigation limits (how far offshore you're covered) and layup credits
• Tell them about your boating safety course — many carriers discount certified operators

We're not insurance agents — compare quotes yourself and read the policy.
